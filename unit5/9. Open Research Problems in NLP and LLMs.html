Open Research Problems in NLP and LLMs

Modern NLP has progressed from task-specific models such as BERT to large language models capable of generation, reasoning, retrieval, coding, and multimodal understanding. However, many important research problems remain unsolved.

The following topics are particularly important for current NLP and LLM research:

  • Long-context modeling
  • Efficient LLMs
  • Hallucination and factuality
  • Multimodal learning
  • Retrieval and RAG
  • Alignment
  • Domain adaptation

1. Long-Context Modeling

1.1 What is Long Context?

The context window is the amount of information a model can process as part of a single interaction. 

Short Context

Question
   ↓
Small amount of text
   ↓
LLM
   ↓
Answer


Long Context

Question
   +
Book
   +
Research Papers
   +
Code
   +
Conversation History
   ↓
LLM
   ↓
Answer

Increasing the context window allows an LLM to process larger documents, long conversations, repositories, and collections of related information.

1.2 Why is Long Context Difficult?

Transformer self-attention traditionally compares tokens with other tokens in the sequence. As the sequence becomes longer, the amount of attention computation can grow rapidly. 

Sequence length = N

Self-Attention
      ↓
Attention Matrix

N × N

Therefore, very long sequences can require substantial memory and computation.

1.3 Example

10,000 tokens
      ↓
100,000 tokens
      ↓
1,000,000 tokens

A research question is: How can a model effectively use extremely long contexts without requiring prohibitively large computation and memory?

1.4 Important Long-Context Problems

  • Efficient attention
  • Memory usage
  • Position representation
  • Retrieval from distant parts of a document
  • Maintaining information across long sequences
  • Reducing the "lost in the middle" effect

1.5 Research Direction

Long Document
     ↓
Efficient Attention
     ↓
Memory Mechanism
     ↓
Relevant Information
     ↓
Accurate Answer

Possible research directions include sparse attention, local/global attention, memory mechanisms, improved positional representations, and retrieval-based approaches.

2. Efficient LLMs

2.1 Why Efficient LLMs?

Large models can contain billions of parameters and require substantial computational resources for training and inference. 

Larger Model
     ↓
More Parameters
     ↓
More Computation
     ↓
Higher Memory Requirement
     ↓
Higher Cost

This creates an important research problem: How can we obtain strong performance with substantially fewer computational resources?

2.2 Efficient LLM Research Areas

  • Model compression
  • Quantization
  • Knowledge distillation
  • Pruning
  • Parameter-efficient fine-tuning
  • Efficient attention
  • Mixture-of-Experts
  • Efficient inference

2.3 Quantization

Quantization represents model parameters using lower-precision numerical formats. 

FP32
 ↓
FP16 / BF16
 ↓
INT8
 ↓
INT4

The objective is to reduce memory and computational requirements while maintaining acceptable model quality.

2.4 Knowledge Distillation

Large Teacher Model
        ↓
     Knowledge
        ↓
Small Student Model
        ↓
Efficient Model

The student attempts to learn useful behavior from a larger teacher model.

2.5 LoRA and PEFT

Large Pretrained Model
        ↓

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning is an important approach when adapting large models to specific tasks.

2.6 Research Question

How can we reduce the memory, latency, energy consumption, and cost of LLMs without causing unacceptable degradation in quality?

3. Hallucination and Factuality

3.1 What is Hallucination?

An LLM can generate fluent text containing claims that are unsupported, fabricated, or inconsistent with available evidence. 

Question
   ↓
LLM
   ↓
Fluent Response
   ↓
Unsupported Claim
   ↓
Hallucination

3.2 Example

Question:
Who wrote a particular book?

Model:
The book was written by Person A in 1995.

Reality:
The book was written by Person B in 2001.

The generated response may sound confident even though its factual claim is incorrect.

3.3 Why Does Hallucination Occur?

A language model is fundamentally trained to model patterns in sequences. Producing a fluent continuation does not automatically guarantee that every generated statement is factually verified.

Potential contributing factors include:

  • Incomplete or noisy training data
  • Ambiguous questions
  • Insufficient knowledge
  • Generation uncertainty
  • Weak grounding in external evidence
  • Long-context retrieval failures

3.4 Reducing Hallucination

Question
   ↓
Retrieve Evidence
   ↓
Provide Evidence to LLM
   ↓
Generate Answer
   ↓
Verify Claims

This motivates approaches such as retrieval-augmented generation, grounded generation, tool use, verification, and improved evaluation.

3.5 Research Questions

  • How can hallucinations be detected automatically?
  • How can unsupported claims be prevented?
  • How can models express uncertainty appropriately?
  • How can generated claims be verified against evidence?
  • How should factuality be measured across domains?

4. Multimodal Learning

4.1 What is Multimodal Learning?

Multimodal learning combines information from multiple modalities. 

Text ───────┐
            │
Image ──────┤
            │
Audio ──────┤
            ↓
      Multimodal Model
            ↓
         Output

A multimodal system may understand relationships between text, images, audio, video, and other forms of information.

4.2 Example

Image:
[Picture of a dog]

Question:
"What is the animal doing?"

             ↓

Vision-Language Model

             ↓

"The dog is running."

4.3 Major Research Challenges

  • Aligning representations across modalities
  • Combining visual and textual information
  • Multimodal reasoning
  • Understanding complex scenes
  • Temporal reasoning over video
  • Reducing visual hallucination
  • Efficient multimodal architectures

4.4 Multimodal Alignment

Image Representation
        ↓
        ├──────► Shared Representation
        ↑
Text Representation

The model must learn that different representations can refer to the same concept.

4.5 Research Question

How can models jointly reason over text, images, audio, video, and other modalities while preserving the information specific to each modality?

6. Alignment

6.1 What is Alignment?

In LLM research, alignment broadly concerns making model behavior better match desired instructions, objectives, constraints, and human preferences. 

Pretrained Model
       ↓
Instruction Tuning
       ↓
Preference / Feedback Training
       ↓
More Instruction-Following Behavior

6.2 Pretraining vs Alignment

Pretraining Alignment / Post-training
Learn general language patterns Improve behavior for desired interactions
Usually uses very large corpora Often uses curated instruction or preference data
Next-token prediction is central Instruction following and preference objectives can be used

6.3 Instruction Tuning

Instruction Dataset

Input:
"Summarize this paragraph."

Output:
"Short summary..."

        ↓

Supervised Fine-Tuning

        ↓

Instruction-Following Model

6.4 Preference Optimization

A preference dataset may contain multiple candidate responses and an indication of which response is preferred under a defined criterion. 

Prompt
  ↓
Response A ──┐
             ├── Preference Signal
Response B ──┘
  ↓
Post-training
  ↓
Model

6.5 Open Alignment Problems

  • How should preferences be represented?
  • How can models follow instructions reliably?
  • How can conflicting instructions be handled?
  • How can undesirable behavior be reduced?
  • How can alignment be evaluated?
  • How can models remain useful while respecting constraints?

7. Domain Adaptation

7.1 What is Domain Adaptation?

A general language model may perform differently when applied to a specialized domain such as medicine, law, finance, agriculture, or science.

          ↓
Domain Data
          ↓
Adaptation
          ↓

7.2 Example

          ↓

"What is a cell?"

          ↓

7.3 Domain Adaptation Methods

  • Continued pretraining
  • Supervised fine-tuning
  • Instruction tuning
  • LoRA / PEFT
  • Domain-specific RAG
  • Domain-specific vocabulary or tokenization research

7.4 Continued Pretraining

A pretrained model can be further trained on domain-specific text before task-specific fine-tuning. 

General Pretraining
       ↓

       ↓

       ↓

       ↓

Useful for learning domain-specific behavior or patterns Useful for accessing external knowledge at inference time
Requires training Requires retrieval infrastructure
Knowledge becomes part of adapted model parameters Knowledge remains in the external knowledge source

11. A Useful Research Framework

When identifying an NLP/LLM research problem, it is useful to separate the problem into four components: 

1. Problem
      ↓
What limitation exists?

2. Method
      ↓
What new architecture / algorithm / training strategy
could address it?

3. Evaluation
      ↓
How will improvement be measured?

4. Ablation
      ↓
Which component actually caused the improvement?

Example

Problem:
RAG retrieves irrelevant documents.

        ↓

Proposed Method:
Improved retrieval + reranking

        ↓

Evaluation:
Recall@K
MRR
Answer accuracy
Faithfulness

        ↓

Ablation:
Baseline Retriever
       vs
Improved Retriever
       vs
Improved Retriever + Reranker

13. Key Takeaway

The major open problems can be viewed as different ways of improving the capabilities and reliability of modern language models: 

Long Context
    → Process more information

Efficient LLMs
    → Reduce computation and memory

Hallucination
    → Improve factual reliability

Multimodal Learning
    → Understand multiple modalities

Retrieval
    → Access external knowledge

Alignment
    → Improve desired model behavior

Domain Adaptation
    → Specialize models for particular domains

These areas provide a foundation for identifying research problems in modern NLP and LLM systems. A strong research project usually focuses on a specific limitation, proposes a measurable improvement, and validates that improvement through appropriate baselines, experiments, and ablation studies.
